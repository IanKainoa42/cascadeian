  • Goal. Default objective is Team Wreckmatch: each takedown scores 1 point. When a round ends, every surviving trooper scores 1 more; if nobody has reached 21 after those survivor points, every squad reforms and another complete round begins. First to 21 after a round wins. Zomboids survival is free on the Objective step; CTF, Raid, and Red Rover are Pro.
  • Command. Battles run in two-minute waves. When a wave ends the sim auto-pauses into a 15-second Regroup (the outer ring cascades away during it — the crumble is the countdown) — a small countdown strip floats over the top of the battlefield (visible in fullscreen too) with a Ready button, but it doesn't block the board: the whole map stays clickable so you can keep planning routes and coverage right through the timeout. Hit Ready (or wait out the clock) to launch the next wave. You can still Pause/Run freely mid-wave.
  • Deploy. Every squad starts in its own glowing corner hex. Select a trooper, then tap or drag to any open small hex inside that boundary. You cannot deploy into the rest of the arena. In hotseat or multiplayer, each human commander readies their squad; battle starts automatically when everyone is ready.
  • Start battle. Step 1 sets match format, objective, movement mode, and turn style. Step 2 picks squad and opponents. Step 3 builds the battlefield. DEPLOY opens formation setup; START BATTLE launches wave 1 after every required commander is ready.
  • Vision. No god's-eye toggle -- fog applies as long as your side is still in the fight. Every teammate contributes its local radius plus wherever its weapon actually has line of sight; the trooper you have selected sees a little further and clearer than the rest. Setting your focus point or planning a route doesn't reveal anything by itself -- only real sightlines do. A gunshot briefly reveals the shooter's hex even through fog, since a shot is something you'd actually notice. Once your whole squad is wiped, or if it's AI-vs-AI with no human seat at all, fog lifts entirely — nothing left to hide from.
  • Camera. The default view frames your squad with a couple of hexes of context instead of showing the whole arena. Use the battle menu to zoom out to the full map when you need the larger read.
  • Board colors. Red hexes are enemy fire coverage or dangerous move destinations. Dark covered hexes are fog — and fog only lifts along real sightlines: nothing is ever seen through a wall (a fired gun briefly pings its muzzle hex, that's the one exception). Pulsing green hexes are safe reachable moves — a white line with arrows shows the order once you set one. Black/white marked hard cover is anchored: it blocks sight, cannot be shoved, and never breaks.
  • Troopers. RF = Rifle, SN = Sniper, MG = SMG, SH = Shield, BR = Burst. Each role also has a different silhouette.
  • Squad. Pick a loadout on the Squad step: Duo (2), Trio (3: SMG, Rifle, Burst -- a small elite fireteam, Rainbow Six-style), Quad (4), Fireteam (5), Breach (5), or Custom (6, Pro).
  • Facing turns, it doesn't snap. A unit's cone rotates toward wherever it's told to look at a limited rate (about a second for a full turn) instead of jumping instantly. Its real, currently-aimed cone (which is what combat and vision actually use) can lag behind its intent for a moment -- that's your window to catch someone mid-turn. Troopers defend themselves, but they can only notice what is actually inside their cone — or standing right on top of them (one hex, two if braced). Nothing outside that cone is ever spotted — no matter how long it stands there or how close it walks. But the cone is not parked: a trooper holding position sweeps it across the approaches it thinks are dangerous, so what is hidden now can be looked at a moment later. Staying out of the lanes a squad is actually watching is a real way in; it buys you a window, not a cloak. Something that does enter a cone takes about half a second to register, slower while marching and slower still sprinting — then the body swings around, takes a beat to steady the aim, and opens up. Once noticed, eyes track it until line of sight breaks, and then it is forgotten and must be re-spotted. Getting shot skips all of it: the hit itself says where the shooter is.
  • Auto-defense has a flinch. Getting hit costs a brief beat (about a third of a second) before a unit can fire back, on top of its normal reload. A surprise shot buys you a real head start.
  • Spray weapons. The SMG and Burst fire several rounds per trigger pull instead of one clean shot. Each round independently picks a target among whoever's in cone right now, weighted by how easy a shot it is -- one enemy in the open eats the whole volley, several enemies means the rounds spread across them. Burst is an actual rapid burst — its rounds leave ~0.1s apart (brrt), it reloads quickly, and its spray carries deep into its long range; the SMG is a fast, wide, close-quarters spray.
  • Facing. Each unit snaps to one of 12 aim positions around the hex, covering sides and corners instead of only six lanes.
  • The arena shrinks. In the last 15 seconds of every wave the doomed outer ring glows amber. Then, during the 15-second regroup, it cascades away hex by hex around the circle — the crumbling ring IS the timer: when the cascade completes, the next wave begins. A trooper caught on a crumbling hex scrambles inward to the nearest safe hex (orders dropped, awareness reset — landing disoriented is the price of dawdling); CTF home markers and a loose flag slide in with it. Sight and shots still cross the chasm — only feet can't. Regroup timeouts tighten as the arena shrinks (15s down to 6s). Ordinary barriers are much tougher now: every stray round that strikes one chips it, and enough sustained fire (dozens of hits) crumbles it. Anchored black/white hard cover never crumbles. The field tightens every wave down to a small final arena, so claim ground that will still exist.
  • Coverage. Each weapon has its own cone: snipers watch a narrow long slice, rifles hold a medium lane, and SMG/shield units cover wider short arcs. A marching unit's cone narrows a little — still open, but holding position aims better.
  • Line of sight is a real, unobstructed line. A trooper can't see, notice, or shoot an enemy unless there's a clear direct line between them — a wall (or another body) sitting on that line blocks it entirely, no peeking around the corner and no shooting through it. Once you do have a line, cover grazing the target's body still trims the odds: if a wall edge hides most of them, the shot is capped near that fraction no matter how good the angle or range otherwise looks. Being in range is not enough — you need the line.
  • Barriers are furniture. Tap an ordinary wall adjacent to your selected trooper to shoulder it one hex forward and take its place — it costs exactly one march step, the same as walking. You end up a hex further forward with that wall now beside you, so shoving cover is how you advance into a firefight. Build your own cover line, or push a wall into a firing lane. The enemy does this too. Anchored hard cover is fixed in place.
  • Cover is directional. A wall only counts as cover if it's actually on the side facing the shooter — tucking in behind a bunker works, but it won't save you from a flank or a shot from your unprotected side. Stand out in the open with no cover and no allies nearby and you're now hit almost every trigger pull someone gets a bead on you — don't linger in open ground.
  • Walls steady the gun — and they stack. Accuracy scales with how many walls are adjacent to the trooper firing: one wall is +10%, four is +50%. Fire rate tapers the same way — one wall reloads about 17% faster, three walls a full 50%. Holding any wall is a fortified posture: +1 vision (you see further from cover), quicker reactions (you notice and fire back faster), and the wall shields the covered side. The flip side — a trooper caught in the open shoots worse (up to −28% accuracy, nothing to brace against), so fighting from cover beats trading in the open.
  • Accuracy is your grouping, not a dice roll. No shot is ever decided by a hidden roll — every round is a real object that flies its line and hits whatever it actually crosses. What accuracy changes is how tight the cone it leaves in: a well-set-up shot goes almost exactly where it was aimed, a bad one wanders a few degrees and sails past. So range, walls, standing still, a wingman on your shoulder and catching someone unaware all pay off as a tighter group — and a round that misses you keeps going into whoever is behind you.
  • Aiming is mostly automatic. A marching trooper looks straight down its heading, out to its weapon's range; a trooper holding position watches the open approaches instead of the ground -- it ranks the lanes an enemy could come down and sweeps its cone across the most dangerous ones (a sniper commits to one, a rifle covers two, an SMG fans three). Any trooper swings its cone onto an enemy the instant it notices one and opens fire — you don't have to aim to defend yourself. Tap outside the green move surround to override and point a specific trooper's line of sight where you want (watch a flank, pre-aim a doorway); it holds that watch until it spots a threat. The line-of-sight focus draws as a fine amber dotted line ending in an eye mark. Only the selected unit's coverage renders; angled peeks can light partial triangle wedges instead of a whole hex.
  • Movement. Real-time uses the pulsing green walking range: tap a green hex to move or drag through it to trace a route. Teleport is its own mode: the same range becomes warp range and movement instantly tunnels to the destination instead of marching. Every warp has a magenta recharge ring, and each extra hex makes that cooldown longer. Turn-based keeps its move-and-action economy.
  • The burst. Immediate moves are a full-tilt sprint — the trooper covers the green step fast and is much harder to put a round on (a sprinter widens the shooter's cone, and can outrun the bullet off the hex entirely), but can't fire during the dash. There's no separate sprint control; short repositioning is intentionally quick, while longer advances come from chaining steps under fire.
  • Move together and you move faster. A trooper with a squadmate nearby has a shorter move cooldown, and it reaches out three hexes — you don't have to be shoulder to shoulder, just travelling as a group. It's strongest up close and fades with distance: one hex away cuts the cooldown by 30%, two hexes by 20%, three hexes by 10%, and past that nothing. Only your nearest teammate counts, so a loose formation moves exactly as fast as a huddle — this rewards keeping the squad together, not stacking it up. Drift out of reach mid-route and the bonus is gone on the next hex. Teleport counts too: a warp's recharge is cut the same way, and it takes whichever end of the jump is better — so blinking out from beside your squad or blinking back to it both pay off, while a lone trooper jumping from nowhere to nowhere pays full.
  • Cohesion — a sweet spot, not "more is always better." Speed and protection read the group differently: move speed only asks how close your nearest squadmate is (out to three hexes), while protection counts who is actually adjacent and peaks at two. Stack up 4+ deep and the protection tapers off and then goes negative — you're in each other's way and an easy cluster to hit, on top of literally not being able to fire through a teammate. A nearby Shield unit gives everyone around it extra protection beyond a normal trooper, offsetting its own short range. Ally links render as a faint dotted mesh behind your move/focus lines so they don't compete for attention.
  • Flanking pays in geometry, not in odds. Coming in from outside a trooper's facing arc doesn't nudge a hit percentage — it means its armour ring hasn't swung a fresh plate onto your line yet, so your rounds meet whatever is already worn down or bare. A flanking hit's tracer flashes magenta and is tagged FLANK instead of the usual pale gold. Shooting someone who hasn't noticed you at all is the bigger prize: an ambush shot leaves the barrel with a far tighter cone.
  • Self-defense. Units fire automatically when enemies enter their coverage cone.
  • Cooldown rings. The gold ring sweeping clockwise is move cooldown; the orange ring sweeping the other way is fire cooldown. Both can show at once.
  • Tracers. Every shot sends a bright laser segment floating from shooter to target — the farther the shot, the longer it takes to arrive, so a sniper's bolt gives you real time to see it coming.
  • Bullets travel for real. A shot's hit/miss doesn't land the instant it's fired — damage only applies once the tracer actually reaches the target hex. Sprint or step off that hex before it arrives and the shot whiffs, no matter how good the odds looked at the moment of firing.
  • Damage. Hits chip health; a unit is removed at 0 HP. Snipers and close SMGs/bursts hit harder; rifles hit hard (2 damage) out to 3 hexes — the steady heavy hitter; shields survive longer but only carry a weak sidearm. Snipers reload slower than the other roles and fumble badly up close (35-60% accuracy inside 2 hexes) -- their range and accuracy come at a real close-quarters cost.
  • Map fairness. Every squad is guaranteed at least a small bunker in front of its spawn. Distinct wall clusters stay separated instead of fusing into one mega-wall, some black/white hard-cover blocks anchor the center and bunkers, and a couple of narrow winding corridors get carved into each map to crawl through.
  • Hit math. Adjacent shots are near certain. Snipers keep accuracy at range with narrow coverage, rifles are balanced, SMGs are broader and close, and shields have a weak wide sidearm cone.
  • Controls. Tap a trooper to select it (or use the bottom roster dock — tap a chip, or ⟩⟩ to cycle). Tap or drag inside the pulsing green range to move; in Teleport mode that command becomes an instant warp and the range disappears until the distance-scaled recharge completes. Tap outside the range to aim its eyes down that lane. Tap an adjacent ordinary wall to shoulder it forward. In real time, the selected card's ⊙ GUARD button pins its line of sight. ▶ SUPPRESS hoses the selected trooper's lane for ten seconds — anyone caught crossing it is PINNED (halted where they stand; falling back is still allowed) — then needs a ten-second recharge. ⇄ swaps the trooper's firing shoulder, which side of cover the muzzle clears. In turn-based play, tap-to-aim and shoulder flip are both free and are the only sight controls. On a phone in landscape the trooper cards dock to the right edge so the board keeps the full height. The same gestures work on a finger, an Apple Pencil, and a Mac trackpad or mouse.
  • The neutral zone. In Teleport and Turn-based, every trooper claims a small personal bubble (by default, just the adjacent ring). An enemy that steps into it has encroached — tap that adjacent enemy to punt it straight down the line it came in on (walls, bodies and the board edge stop the slide early) and briefly stun it. Standing outside the bubble is always legal, however far someone travelled to get there — only stepping in is the infraction.
  • Red Rover is a corner-running game. Every squad starts inside its own seven-cell corner hex. Runners score by reaching a defending corner; defenders must move outside their own glow to fight. Every board hex remains traversable, but you cannot finish a route or fire from your own end zone. Nobody opens fire on their own — aim at a target, or return fire when attacked. The point is getting through, not winning a deathmatch — set Engagement: Free fire in Advanced for the old shoot-on-sight.
  • Turn-based uses the same physical shots as Teleport. The marker over a target previews what the committed ray meets: WALL, CLEAR, another unit intercepting the lane, or the target's PLATE, BODY, or exposed CORE. Accuracy tightens or widens that launch line; it never rolls hit versus miss. The same positions, bearing, range, armour orientation, muzzle, and aim always produce the same ray. A plate absorbs its configured toughness, then shatters; repeat fire through that opening reaches the body shard and finally the core.
  • Turn-based is simultaneous. Every participating player plans up to 3 actions from the same round-start state, then all submitted movement and fire play together through the live simulation. The shared round resolves when its 20-second timer expires, or earlier when every player is out of actions. READY locks a plan early; an idle or disconnected online player cannot hold the round open. The replay lifts the fog so everyone can watch the physical trajectories and impacts, then planning resumes with each side's own sight information.
  • AI skill. On the Squad step: Recruit, Regular or Veteran. It does not touch the dice -- every trooper's hit chance is identical at all three. What changes is how well the enemy is run: how often it re-tasks its squad, how many troopers it can redirect at once, how long it must hold you in view before it may fire, and how quickly it reacts once it has. Veteran squads flow; Recruit squads are slow to notice you and slower to answer. Real-time only -- turn-based runs a different AI.
  • Service record. Your wins, losses, kills and best wave reached are kept on this device and shown under the wordmark on the home screen. Zomboids never counts as a loss -- it is endless, so it logs your best wave instead. Nothing is uploaded anywhere; Reset record at the bottom of this guide wipes it.
  • Blast knockback. Optional and experimental: when a unit drops to 0 HP, the impact shoves adjacent enemies one hex away if there is room.
  • Multiplayer. Create a room to get a 4-letter code, then tap SHARE INVITE — friends open the link or type the code and appear in your lobby. Whoever joins takes the next open team (up to as many teams as the Format allows; the rest stay AI); the host starts the battle when everyone's in. The host is the only device actually running the battle -- everyone else sends move/focus/ready requests and watches the result. If a phone lock or app switch kills the link, both sides notice within seconds, the joiner auto-rejoins the same room, and until they return their team plays as AI. A room chat is built in. Turn-based works online too: every player plans the shared simultaneous round on their own device; the host owns the 20-second deadline and resolves the submitted plans through the same physical simulation for everyone.
  • Event feed. The scrolling panel above the field logs recent shots, hits, and captures, not just the single latest line in the status bar.
